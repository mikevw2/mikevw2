# Beyond consulting and software: fresh ideas for Mike Van Wagenen

Date: 2026-10-08. Follows `BUSINESS_FIT.md`. Additional checks run today on local labs, calibration providers, contract sanitation, equipment dealers, staffing, aseptic capacity, and auditor paths. Figures marked "estimate" are industry-typical and were not verified for Idaho.

## Five takes that reframe the question

1. **Your moat is physical presence, not code.** You can walk onto a wet floor at 2 a.m. and be trusted. Nobody in Boise or Idaho Falls can. Every idea below requires a truck, and that is the point: AI cannot drive to Jerome.
2. **The cluster's binding constraints are labor, lab turnaround, and third shift.** None of those are software problems. The research found no pathogen lab, no accredited calibration provider, and no named contract sanitation firm that lists Twin Falls.
3. **Compliance creates calendars, and calendars are recurring revenue without SaaS.** Calibration cycles, swab schedules, pre-op verification, re-certification dates. A service tied to a regulatory cadence churns like software and sells like a handshake.
4. **Software is your margin lever, not your product.** Test for "game changing": would a plant pay for the outcome even if your software were invisible? If yes, the software makes you cheaper and stickier than the next guy. If no, it is a hobby.
5. **Get paid to do discovery.** You have never seen 40 plants' problems in a year. Auditors do.

## The ideas

### 1. An independent verification company (lead recommendation)

What it is: a field crew that performs and documents everything an auditor asks to see records for. Instrument calibration and verification (thermometers, scales, pH meters, ATP meters, metal-detector and checkweigher challenges, recorder charts), environmental monitoring swabbing on a zoned map with courier to a lab, pre-op ATP verification, and trending with corrective-action escalation. Start as one person and a van. Add accreditation later.

Why it fits: no ISO 17025 calibration provider lists Twin Falls; Fox Valley Metrology serves Idaho from Boise with 3 to 5 day turnaround. Deibel has a Twin Falls lab and Eurofins is in Idaho Falls, so you do not need to own a lab; you own the plant-side work nobody staffs. 86% of environmental monitoring programs run on paper or spreadsheets. SQF Edition 10 tightens environmental monitoring and scoring from January 2027. Costco requires 60 days of records or automatic failure. Your floor knowledge tells you which instruments matter and where Listeria hides. Your software becomes the instrument registry, swab map, trending and evidence export, invisible to the buyer and impossible for a Boise cal house to match.

|---|---|---|
| Startup kit (reference thermometers, dry block, bath, test weights, ATP luminometer) | $30k to $80k | estimate |
| ISO 17025 accreditation, if pursued | $20k to $40k, 12 to 18 months | estimate |

Brutal part: metrology is a discipline, not a vibe. You will need to learn uncertainty budgets and traceability, and some plants will insist on accredited certificates from day one. Local revenue caps around $200k to $500k unless you extend to Boise and Cache Valley. It is a van business until it is a crew business.

### 2. Contract sanitation with verified records

What it is: third-shift sanitation crews for mid-tier plants, bilingual, with pre-op verification and a master sanitation schedule delivered as records, not promises.

Why it fits: Fortrex, the former PSSI, is the national giant and its only Idaho site found is in Kuna, with a WARN notice for 53 layoffs effective July 30, 2026. Mid-tier Magic Valley plants post their own sanitation jobs at $22 to $29 an hour and cannot fill them. Sanitation plus CIP is your actual trade. A single 100-person plant's sanitation budget is a company on its own.

| Item | Figure | Status |
|---|---|---|
| Sanitation spend, 100-person plant, per year | $500k to $1.5M | estimate |
| Contract sanitation margin | 8% to 15% | estimate |

Brutal part: this is the hardest business on the list. Payroll before receivables, workers' comp, I-9 and child-labor scrutiny after the PSSI scandal, 3 a.m. no-shows, and chemical injuries. Your software is a footnote. But it is the largest pool of money in the cluster that AI will never touch, and one signed plant makes you a real employer.

### 3. Buy a service business that already touches the plants

What it is: acquire an aging owner-operator firm in food-adjacent industrial services. Candidates: calibration, pest management for food plants, refrigeration and ammonia service, sanitary stainless fabrication, industrial cleaning, a small testing lab.

Why it fits: Main Street firms trade at roughly 2.0 to 2.6 times seller discretionary earnings, median price about $350k, and SBA 7(a) under the June 2025 SOP needs a 10% equity injection, half of which can be a standby seller note. You bring CI and systems to a business with customers, something you have never had.

Brutal part: no Idaho food-services listings surfaced; you would hunt off-market through SIEDO and the SBDC. You must leave your job at close. You have never run a P&L. Key-person discounts of 20 to 40% exist because clients follow the seller out the door.

### 4. Become a contract SQF or BRCGS auditor for 12 to 18 months

What it is: not a business. A paid reconnaissance mission. Certification bodies hire contract auditors paid per audit and salaried auditors at roughly $90k to $105k. Requirements seen in postings: 5 years in food, HACCP training, a 40-hour lead auditor course, and in some cases a degree.

Why it fits: you would see 40 to 60 plants a year, learn exactly what fails, and build the only network that matters, all while someone else pays for it. Every later idea gets sharper.

Brutal part: heavy travel, and a lead auditor cannot consult for or sell to plants they audit. Your README does not show a food science degree; some bodies require one.

### 5. Bilingual QA technician academy plus staffing

What it is: a 6 to 8 week Spanish-first "food QA tech" program with CSI or TechHelp, feeding a temp-to-hire staffing agency.

Why it fits: plants post QA techs at $46k to $62k and cannot fill them. Express Employment places QA techs at $22 an hour in Logan. SQF requires competency records for anyone covering a food safety role; your training system is the record. Staffing is revenue times markup, not hours of your life.

Brutal part: staffing is cash-hungry and low margin, and Gem State Staffing and Express already exist. The academy only matters if a plant commits to hiring graduates before you enroll anyone.

### Ideas I looked at and would not pursue

- **Acid whey valorization.** Chobani solved its Twin Falls problem with reverse osmosis in 2014; the remaining problem is capital-intensive process chemistry, not yours.
- **Used dairy equipment brokerage.** No Idaho dealer exists, which is tempting, but 3-A and PMO certification risk on used fillers and tanks puts you between an auction house and a lawsuit.
- **Shared-use processing facility.** ISDA funded a community kitchen in Salmon through RFSI; this is a 3 to 5 year economic-development project with grant cycles, not an owner's business.
- **Aseptic co-packing.** Capacity is a real constraint and you sit on an aseptic floor, but entry is $50M plus. The only version you can own is the startup and validation crew for new aseptic lines, which is project services again.

## What I would do in your position

Build the verification company. Start with calibration and environmental monitoring field work at two non-dairy plants, priced per instrument and per swab on a monthly schedule. Keep ProcedureIQ and the Plant Ops Hub tracker as the invisible back office. Add pre-op verification in month six. When a plant asks you to just take over the whole third shift, that is the signal to decide whether you want the sanitation company. If you cannot land two verification contracts by March 2027, the cluster is telling you something, and no idea on this list will fix that.
